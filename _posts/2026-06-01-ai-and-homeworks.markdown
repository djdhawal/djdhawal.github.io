I seem to have lucked into graduate school at a time when the technical landscape is shifting rapidly. As I work through problem sets, homework, and final projects, I have come to realize that neither the grading rubrics nor the assignments themselves have kept up with the times.

The problem

Homework is meant to check whether students are keeping up with the course material. I don't think conventional homework can do that anymore. Given the abilities of today's state-of-the-art large language models (LLMs), I haven't come across a single homework problem recently that a free AI chatbot couldn't solve in one prompt. Meanwhile, students are juggling a social life, part-time jobs to pay their bills, and the exhausting grind of job applications. Solving homework in one prompt frees up hours for everything else while still earning decent grades. The incentive, then, is to maximize GPA, and that is now possible without actually learning the material. For the first time, students can avoid the old trade-off of sacrificing their social lives for their studies.

Most people already recognize that GPA is becoming an increasingly unreliable measure of intellectual ability. Under the current system, the only students who will still choose to learn are those who genuinely care about the subject: they enjoy its language and value the knowledge they gain from it. For these students, nothing has changed. They believe education should do more than turn them into memory machines or retrieval systems, and that learning something will change the way they think. But a system that relies on intrinsic motivation alone is failing everyone else. That is why the homework itself needs to change.

What I propose

Homework should present real problems, not toy tasks.
It should be done in a shared space by a group of students, with roles and a team structure set by the professor, much like an engineering team at a tech company.
It should be done during class. At that point it wouldn't really be "homework," and grading could be immediate.

I believe this approach would help students in two ways. First, working on real problems encourages AI-assisted learning done well. While building something, students learn to judge which knowledge they need to retain themselves and which they can leave to AI. Second, working in groups makes students aware of how much the team as a whole knows, and it shows them more clearly the gaps in their own knowledge that AI can't fill. Professors and instructors can contribute a great deal here by sharing insights from their own experience: things AI-generated answers tend to miss or often get wrong.

This approach would also keep the whole class on the same page about how to use AI and how its abilities are changing. That matters more and more, because it is easy to fall behind and fail to use this technology to its full potential. It's like having a 1 Gbps internet connection and using it only to read text messages.

This way of teaching could also improve interdisciplinary learning. Economics and math classes, for example, could pair up and tackle problems at their intersection as group assignments. That could lead to broader learning and a more holistic way of developing ideas.
